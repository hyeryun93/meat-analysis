Q. Were the two pH values at each time point measured repeatedly from the same two samples over time, or were two different samples measured at each time point?

##case 1 Suppose the same samples are measured repeatedly over time.

For example, the first sample measured at Time 1 is the same sample measured at Time 2, Time 4, and so on.

Because repeated measurements come from the same sample, the observations may be correlated rather than fully independent.

For example, a sample with a relatively high pH at one time point may also tend to have a relatively high pH at later time points.

Therefore, a simple linear model that assumes independent observations may not be appropriate. Depending on the situation, we may need a repeated-measures model, a mixed-effects model, or a model that explicitly accounts for the correlation structure.

If the p-value is smaller than the significance level \(\alpha\), we reject \(H_0\) = we reject the hypothesis that the quadratic term is unnecessary.

=The very small p-value indicates that adding the quadratic term substantially improves the fit compared with the simple linear model.

The key point is that the slope \(\beta_1\) is constant everywhere. \[ \text{pH} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Time} \]

Once this term is included, the slope no longer has to stay constant. In other words, the rate of decline is allowed to change over time. \[ \text{pH} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Time} + \beta_2 \text{Time}^2 \]

So the curvature test is essentially asking: Do we need \(\beta_2\)?

ANOVA(Analysis of Variance) “After adding the quadratic term, did the unexplained variation decrease enough?”

-> “What model can account for both of these features?”

“mixed-effects model with a quadratic time effect”

\[ \text{pH}_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\text{Time}_{ij} + \beta_2\text{Time}_{ij}^2 + b_i + \epsilon_{ij} \] A mixed-effects model combines the overall common pattern with sample-specific differences.

It is called mixed because it combines fixed effects and random effects.

  • Fixed effect = Time → This is the overall effect we are interested in.
  • Random effect = Sample → This represents natural differences among samples.

We compared a simple linear regression model with a quadratic regression model.

RSS measures the amount of unexplained variation remaining after fitting the model. -> Quadratic RSS = 0.14: The quadratic model leaves much less unexplained variation than the linear model.

But adding another predictor almost always reduces RSS. -> The F-test(Fisher’s F statistic) is used to determine whether the null hypothesis should be rejected.

\[ F = \frac{\text{Extra variation explained by }Time^2} {\text{Residual variation}} \]

small pvalue: reject the null hypothesis and conclude that the quadratic term should be included in the model.

\[ H_0:\beta_2=0 \]
